## 1. Mature-node chip foundry (28nm+)
**Paper:** [Multilevel AlScN Ferroelectric Diode Crossbar Arrays with Sub-200 nm Cells for Selector-Free Nonvolatile Memory](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21381) — Kwan-Ho Kim et al.
**Pipeline:** New switching or state-storage mechanism -> Logic and memory
**Readiness score:** 10/10 — weakest: operating_conditions (Room temperature nonvolatile memory operation; no cryogenics required.)
**What they found:** Scientists built a tiny memory chip using a special crystal material (AlScN) that can store three bits of data in a single cell by flipping its electrical polarization, like flipping a tiny magnet. Each cell is unbelievably small, about 200 nanometers across (you could fit hundreds across the width of a human hair), and uses hardly any energy to switch - about half a trillionth of a joule per write. They packed 128 by 128 of these cells into a working 2-kilobyte memory grid without needing extra selector switches to prevent data crosstalk.
**Why it matters for Mature-node chip foundry (28nm+):** Computer chip makers need a way to pack more memory into less space while cutting down the extra circuitry (selectors) that take up room and power. If this crystal-based memory can be made reliably, it could let chipmakers build denser, more efficient memory using processes added on top of normal chip manufacturing.
**The catch:** The test only read 283 cells out of the whole array, and the different storage levels were only shown to stay stable for about 15 minutes (though the two most extreme states lasted closer to 14 hours), far short of the years of storage real memory chips need to last.

## 2. Superconducting electronics & detectors
**Paper:** [CREST: A Hardware-Aware GNN-Based Framework for High-Fidelity and Robust Superconducting Quantum Compilation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24886) — Wenjie Sun et al.
**Pipeline:** Theory or model insight -> Design and simulation tools
**Readiness score:** 10/10 — weakest: operating_conditions (GNN framework running on existing hardware calibrations.)
**What they found:** Researchers built a smart computer program that learns which part of a quantum computer chip is best suited to run a particular task. On a real quantum computer with 176 qubits, it made results 19% more accurate than the best previous method, and kept working well even after the chip's calibration settings changed over time.
**Why it matters for Superconducting electronics & detectors:** Superconducting quantum chips are noisy and their quality drifts day to day, so picking the best region of the chip for each job really matters; a tool that automatically picks better regions could make existing quantum hardware produce more trustworthy answers without needing better chips.
**The catch:** This is a software scheduling trick tested on one company's specific 176-qubit chip and circuit set, so it does not fix the underlying noise or scale problems of the superconducting qubits themselves, and it is unclear how well it transfers to other hardware designs.

## 3. Optical fiber & preforms
**Paper:** [Distributed Fiber Monitoring on a Coherent DSP ASIC: Implementation of Longitudinal Power Monitoring](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25353) — Takeo Sasai et al.
**Pipeline:** New measurement technique -> Inspection and quality control
**Readiness score:** 10/10 — weakest: reproducibility (Validated on 1005 km links with multi-vendor signals)
**What they found:** Engineers built a feature into a communication chip that already sits at the end of fiber-optic cables, so it can tell not just how strong the signal is overall but exactly where along a 1005-kilometer cable the signal is getting weaker. It works without slowing down the data or using more power, and it even works with equipment made by other companies.
**Why it matters for Optical fiber & preforms:** Fiber optic networks today can tell you a cable has a problem, but not where. This lets the same chips that send internet data also act like a stethoscope for the whole cable, pinpointing trouble spots without adding separate monitoring hardware.
**The catch:** This upgrades the electronics at the ends of the fiber, not the fiber or glass-making process itself, so it helps operators manage existing cables rather than changing how fiber is produced.

## 4. Thermoelectric modules
**Paper:** [Prediction of lattice thermal conductivity of half-Heusler compounds by machine learning and transfer-function calibration](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26788) — Shivam Randive et al.
**Pipeline:** Theory or model insight -> Design and simulation tools
**Readiness score:** 10/10 — weakest: operating_conditions (Comparison of theory to existing measurements; computational.)
**What they found:** Scientists compared computer predictions of how well half-Heusler materials block heat to real lab measurements. They found the computer models usually overestimate how well these materials block heat, especially at low temperatures, because real chunks of material have tiny imperfections that stop heat even better than a perfect crystal would. They built a simple correction formula that fixes this gap, making predictions about as accurate as when two different labs measure the same thing and compare notes.
**Why it matters for Thermoelectric modules:** Thermoelectric module makers use these computer searches to find new materials that turn waste heat into electricity, so a correction that removes a systematic bias means fewer wasted experiments chasing materials that looked good on paper but will not perform as well in real devices.
**The catch:** The fix is a statistical patch based on past literature data, not a new material or a new physics model, so it only narrows the gap in predictions and does not tell you why some individual compounds still miss by a lot.

## 5. Fluorspar & hydrofluoric acid
**Paper:** [Dynamics and transport in LiF using classical and machine learned interatomic potentials](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21900) — Harvey L. Devereux et al.
**Pipeline:** Theory or model insight -> Design and simulation tools
**Readiness score:** 10/10 — weakest: operating_conditions (MD simulations; computational method, no special hardware.)
**What they found:** Scientists used computer simulations to study molten lithium fluoride, a salty liquid used in some advanced reactors. They compared old-style physics formulas with newer AI-trained models and found the AI models predicted its stickiness (viscosity) and melting point much closer to real lab measurements.
**Why it matters for Fluorspar & hydrofluoric acid:** Better computer models of molten fluoride salts could help engineers design safer, more efficient equipment that handles these salts, like molten-salt reactors or fluorine chemical processes, without needing as many expensive real-world tests.
**The catch:** This is purely a simulation study on one simple salt; it does not test real industrial fluorspar or hydrofluoric acid processes, so it is unclear how well the method works for more complex, impure real-world materials.

## 6. Fiber & diode lasers
**Paper:** [Dual-Comb Spectrometry Enabled by Gain-Assisted Electro-Optic Fiber Ring Resonators](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.22348) — Yuan Chen et al.  *(unverified — the checking pass failed for this paper)*
**Pipeline:** Light-matter interaction -> Photonics
**Readiness score:** 9/10 — weakest: reproducibility (Single group demonstration; multiple independent samples not stated.)
**What they found:** Scientists built two fiber-optic ring devices that each make a 'comb' of laser colors, like a ruler made of light. By adding extra amplification inside the loop, they got a very wide comb (1.8 THz) and could tune its rhythm from about 5 billion to 200 million ticks per second, while keeping the two combs almost perfectly in step (a tiny 2 Hz mismatch) without needing extra equipment to stabilize them.
**Why it matters for Fiber & diode lasers:** This could make dual-comb spectrometers, which measure gases and chemicals super fast and precisely, simpler, cheaper and more compact by removing the need for bulky stabilization electronics, which matters for fiber-laser based sensing and metrology instruments.
**The catch:** This is a lab demonstration using specialized fiber components and a shared laser source, so it is not yet shown to work outside a controlled optics lab or at mass-manufacturing scale.

## 7. Sputtering targets & thin-film deposition
**Paper:** [Breaking the Fixed-Substrate Limitation of Deep Learning Ellipsometry with Pseudo-Substrate Spectra](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20694) — Yuki Ikarashi et al.
**Pipeline:** New measurement technique -> Inspection and quality control
**Readiness score:** 9/10 — weakest: operating_conditions (not stated)
**What they found:** Scientists made a faster way for computers to figure out how thin a film of material is and how it bends light, using a method called ellipsometry. Normally the computer program has to be retrained for 100 hours every time you change the base material underneath the film, but this new method skips that by also looking at the bare base material's signal. It worked on 47 real samples in under 2 seconds each, matching careful manual measurements closely.
**Why it matters for Sputtering targets & thin-film deposition:** Thin-film factories, like those making coatings for computer chips or glass, need to quickly check film thickness and quality on many different base materials without reprogramming their测试 tools each time.
**The catch:** The system was trained and tested mostly on simple substrates like metal, glass, and SiO2/Si, so it is unclear how well it handles more complicated real factory stacks or unusual materials.

## 8. Alkaline electrolyzers
**Paper:** [AdsNet: A Graph Neural Network for Direct Adsorption Energy Prediction](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25646) — Changzhi Ai et al.
**Pipeline:** Theory or model insight -> Design and simulation tools
**Readiness score:** 9/10 — weakest: reproducibility (Single model training; independent validation not stated.)
**What they found:** Scientists built a computer program that guesses how strongly different chemicals stick to metal surfaces, which is important for designing catalysts. It only looks at a small patch around where the chemical sticks, rather than the whole material, so it works fast and was tested on 33,920 example calculations. Its guesses were off by only about 0.032 electron-volts, which is very close to the true answer.
**Why it matters for Alkaline electrolyzers:** Finding good catalysts for splitting water into hydrogen and oxygen usually takes slow, expensive quantum chemistry calculations for every candidate material. A tool like this could let engineers test thousands of catalyst surfaces for electrolyzers much faster, like trying on shoes with a size-predicting app instead of trying on every pair in the store.
**The catch:** The tool was only trained and tested on 26 metals and 6 simple chemical fragments, so it is unproven for the more complex catalyst mixtures and conditions used in real electrolyzers.

## 9. Indium refining
**Paper:** [From power to spectrum: an off-the-shelf photodiode turned into a spectrometer by its bias alone](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26532) — Roi Yozevitch et al.
**Pipeline:** Light-matter interaction -> Photonics
**Readiness score:** 9/10 — weakest: performance_margin (Spectral resolution 0.3-0.4 nm; comparable to incumbent spectrometers, not superior.)
**What they found:** Scientists took a normal infrared light detector (used in internet fiber-optic cables) and found that if you wiggle its electrical voltage up and down fast, the pattern of the electric current it produces can tell you the color of the light hitting it. Using a computer program, they could tell apart up to three laser colors spread across an 80-nanometer-wide band, locating each color to within about a third of a nanometer, and getting the count of colors right 99% of the time.
**Why it matters for Indium refining:** This turns cheap, everyday light sensors already used to monitor laser power in fiber networks into miniature spectrometers, without needing to add filters, prisms, or special light-splitting chips.
**The catch:** It only works at room temperature on a tiny, well-controlled set of pure laser colors in a lab, not on messy, real-world light with many overlapping colors, and each device needs its own trained computer model to decode the signal.

## 10. Solar PV cells & modules
**Paper:** [A database of optical and thermal-radiative behaviour of materials under solar radiation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21281) — Vasiliki E. Alexopoulou et al.  *(unverified — the checking pass failed for this paper)*
**Pipeline:** Theory or model insight -> Design and simulation tools
**Readiness score:** 9/10 — weakest: performance_margin (Compiles and standardizes existing literature data; not novel measurement.)
**What they found:** Scientists built a database called SpaderOptics that collects how 21 different materials (metals, ceramics, plastics, and more) react to sunlight, like how much light they reflect, absorb, or let through. It also calculates how hot these materials would get under concentrated space sunlight, using a standard called AM0 that mimics sunlight outside Earth's atmosphere.
**Why it matters for Solar PV cells & modules:** Engineers designing solar panels or spacecraft parts could use this database to predict how materials will behave in sunlight without running their own experiments, saving time when choosing materials.
**The catch:** The database only covers solid materials at specific temperatures and only works for sunlight in space, not sunlight that has passed through Earth's atmosphere, so it can't yet be used for most ground-based solar panels.
