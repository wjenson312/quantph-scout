### Highly uniform first-electron position in qubit arrays fabricated on dedicated QSOI(R) 300mm commercial platform

Johan Pelloux-Prayer, Elise Prin, Giselle A. Elbaz, Pierre-Louis Julliard, et al. — [arXiv:2609.20043](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.20043) — TRL 3 — confidence medium — score 2.8

**Finding.** Quobly and STMicroelectronics report a 300mm-CMOS-compatible modified 28nm FD-SOI process ('QSOI') tuned for silicon spin-qubit fabrication. At room temperature, QSOI devices show tighter, more uniform transistor characteristics (threshold voltage, subthreshold swing) across wafers than standard 28nm FD-SOI. At cryogenic temperature (chuck ~900mK, wafer estimated ~2K), wafer-scale testing across 2615 devices found 69% of tested plunger gates (377/546 maps) could detect first-electron loading into a quantum dot via an adjacent SET charge sensor, with the first-electron voltage position highly uniform across 377 measured quantum dots (21 mV ± 35 mV) — which the authors state, but do not benchmark against a specific competing number, is the lowest dispersion of this metric reported at wafer scale. On a directly comparable metric (matched-pair threshold voltage std dev), this work's 101 mV is worse than the 59 mV reported by the cited industrial comparator (Neyens et al. 2024, Intel's 300mm platform).

### Accurate noise spectroscopy using quantum sensing ensembles

Dhilan T Vallury, Nikolai Dontschuk, Alexander M Jakob, Alexander J Healey, et al. — [arXiv:2609.16643](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.16643) — TRL 3 — confidence medium — score 2.1

**Finding.** The authors solved a data-analysis problem for quantum sensor arrays: when a single quantum sensor (e.g. an NV centre in diamond) is replaced by a large ensemble, each sensor sits at a slightly different distance from the noise source, so the decoupling-pulse-sequence timing used to select a probe frequency also silently re-weights which sub-population of sensors dominates the signal. Ignoring this causes systematic errors in the recovered noise spectrum. The paper introduces a fitting procedure that folds in the known (or NV-NMR-measured) depth distribution of the sensor ensemble, and shows in simulation and in a two-ensemble NV-diamond experiment (2 keV and 4 keV implants on one substrate) that this recovers a consistent power-law surface noise spectrum from 1-10 MHz across the two differently shallow ensembles, whereas a naive single-sensor-style inversion mixes depth and frequency effects and can be mistaken for a spurious spectral cut-off.

**Measured**

| Required knowledge of sensor depth-distribution width for accurate reconstruction (simulated) | 10 | percent |
| Measured noise spectrum frequency span (experiment) | 1 to 10 | MHz |
| Residual ratio, Lorentzian fit vs power-law fit to experimental ensemble data | approximately a factor of two | dimensionless |
| Static bias magnetic field applied during spin measurements | 70 | mT |
| Excitation laser power | 250 | mW |

| Natural abundance of 13C causing revival dips in decoherence curves | 1.1 | percent |

**Operating constraints.** Requires shallow NV-diamond ensembles created by ion implantation into a specific 'electronic grade' diamond wafer with a custom acid/Piranha/anneal/UV-ozone surface preparation sequence; Sensing performed on a custom-built widefield microscope with a 532 nm laser, sCMOS camera, and RF signal generation (Rohde & Schwarz SMBV100A + Keysight AWG) delivered via an on-chip gold resonator; Requires a static bias magnetic field of ~70 mT aligned to a specific NV axis; Requires independent NV-NMR measurements to characterise the sensor depth distribution before the noise-spectrum fit can be trusted (accuracy needs to be within about 10% of true width); Analysis validated on only two hand-fabricated ensembles (2 keV and 4 keV implants) on a single diamond substrate, not a manufacturing-scale or repeatable QC process; Requires surface functionalisation with a Fomblin oil layer and reliance on ambient adsorbate water layer as calibration NMR targets; Simulated robustness limits (2% readout noise, 10% distribution-width knowledge) are not yet demonstrated as hard experimental bounds on the reported dataset; The paper itself flags an unresolved confound: a depth-independent 'bulk' noise contribution that may explain a discrepancy between the two ensembles' fitted spectra, which the current method does not model

**Applications vs. incumbents**

- *Quantum hardware engineering* — Characterising MHz-range surface/decoherence noise on batches of shallow NV-diamond qubit or sensor material using wide-field ensembles rather than probing one defect at a time
  - Incumbent: Single-NV noise spectroscopy via confocal dynamical decoupling (as in Romach et al., Phys. Rev. Lett. 114, 017601)
  - Comparison: The paper does not report a magnetic-field sensitivity figure (e.g. T/sqrt(Hz)) for either the ensemble method or a single-NV comparison, so no direct sensitivity number can be compared; the claimed advantage is field-of-view and throughput (measuring many sensors simultaneously) at the cost of an additional depth-distribution calibration step (NV-NMR) that single-NV spectroscopy does not require, and the whole pipeline still runs on a custom widefield microscope, not a portable or turnkey instrument.
- *Quantum hardware engineering* — Characterisation of surface noise sources on diamond substrates used for NV-based sensing devices, as a possible future input to surface treatment or implant-depth decisions
  - Incumbent: Direct interpretation of ensemble coherence decay via the standard single-sensor inversion formula (Eq. 1, Szankowski et al. 2017) without correcting for depth-distribution bias
  - Comparison: The paper shows the naive (incumbent) approach systematically underestimates the noise spectral density and, at a distribution width of 7 nm, produces distortions large enough to be mistaken for a genuine spectral cut-off; the corrected method instead recovers consistent power-law exponents across two ensembles with a residual roughly half that of an incorrect (Lorentzian) spectral-shape guess. However, this is demonstrated on a single substrate with two hand-fabricated implants, each requiring its own dedicated NV-NMR depth calibration, and the authors themselves note an unmodelled bulk-noise contribution that offsets the two ensembles' fitted intensities — so this is not yet a repeatable quality-control diagnostic, only a proof-of-principle comparison.

**Gap to practice.** This is a data-analysis/fitting framework validated on one diamond sample with two implant conditions, not a packaged instrument or a manufacturing process. To become field- or fab-usable it would need: (1) demonstration on more ensembles and other solid-state/molecular sensor platforms to confirm generality beyond NV centres; (2) a faster or automated way to obtain the depth (or coupling) distribution in situ, since accurate calibration (NV-NMR) currently requires its own dedicated measurement per sample; (3) explicit experimental validation of the noise-tolerance and distribution-width-tolerance limits that are so far only established in simulation; (4) a model for the bulk-noise contribution the authors identify as an unresolved confound in their own two-ensemble comparison; (5) integration into a standard, reproducible acquisition/analysis pipeline rather than the current bespoke widefield microscope and manual fitting procedure, before it could function as a repeatable QC diagnostic rather than a single research demonstration.

### Singly resonant InGaP microresonators for efficient, low-threshold second-order nonlinear optics

Yiming Pang, Xuefeng Li, Lucas Wang, Lillian Thiel, et al. — [arXiv:2609.16718](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.16718) — TRL 3 — confidence medium — score 2.1

**Finding.** The authors built a millimeter-scale InGaP-on-insulator microresonator that resonates only the fundamental (telecom) light while the frequency-doubled (second-harmonic, 780 nm) light exits in a single pass — a 'singly resonant' architecture that normally requires centimeter-scale cavities and sacrifices efficiency compared to doubly resonant chip devices. Using InGaP's large second-order nonlinearity combined with high-Q fundamental-mode resonances, they measured up to 14.4 mW of second-harmonic output at 57% absolute conversion efficiency in a 0.96 mm cavity, generated more than 25 sets of time-energy-correlated photon pairs via spontaneous parametric down-conversion (SPDC) with an on-chip pair-generation efficiency up to 0.26 MHz/µW per mode pair, and demonstrated optical parametric oscillation (OPO) with a 0.71 mW threshold in a 2.86 mm cavity — a threshold the authors state is the lowest reported for any chip-scale OPO with a non-resonant pump, in a cavity at least five times shorter than prior PPLN devices. The paper directly claims its singly resonant devices match the conversion efficiency of doubly resonant systems while exceeding their SH output power. Two headline numbers cited elsewhere in the analysis — the exact intrinsic Q-factor and the exact SHG conversion efficiency in %/W — could not be verified because the corresponding figures were missing from the available text.

**Applications vs. incumbents**

- *Quantum hardware engineering* — On-chip source of frequency-multiplexed, time-energy-correlated photon pairs as a building block for future frequency-multiplexed quantum-networking or entanglement-distribution links
  - Incumbent: Doubly resonant AlN/LiNbO3/Si3N4 microring photon-pair sources and periodically poled lithium niobate (PPLN) waveguide sources
  - Comparison: This device achieves an on-chip pair-generation efficiency of up to 0.26 MHz/µW per mode pair with more than 25 correlated mode pairs from a single singly-resonant cavity, avoiding the need to align widely separated fundamental and second-harmonic resonances required in doubly resonant microrings. The paper demonstrates correlated pair generation and joint spectral correlation, not entanglement or a working QKD/networking protocol, and requires an off-chip PPLN SH pump plus cryogenically-cooled SNSPDs for detection; no directly comparable pair-generation efficiency number for a specific doubly resonant incumbent device is given, so a head-to-head efficiency comparison in the same units is not established in the text.
- *Quantum hardware engineering* — Low-threshold, chip-integrated optical parametric oscillator as a widely tunable coherent light source for photonic quantum computing and spectroscopy front-ends
  - Incumbent: Centimeter-long periodically-poled lithium niobate (PPLN) singly-resonant OPOs with non-resonant pump
  - Comparison: The InGaP device reaches an OPO threshold of 0.71 mW in a 2.86 mm cavity, described by the authors as 'the lowest threshold reported for any chip-scale OPO with a non-resonant pump' and achieved in a cavity 'at least five times shorter' than prior PPLN devices, which typically show thresholds of tens of milliwatts (two-to-three orders of magnitude higher than doubly resonant microring OPOs, per the text) — roughly a 10-100x threshold reduction versus the PPLN incumbent, though doubly resonant microring OPOs may still have even lower absolute thresholds not directly quantified here. The demonstration also showed occasional mode-hopping over ~10-minute windows from unstabilized fiber coupling, so turn-key spectroscopy use would need packaging improvements.
- *Quantum hardware engineering* — Broadband, thermally tunable second-harmonic/frequency-doubling module for converting telecom-band light to the 780 nm band used in atomic/ion clock and quantum-memory systems
  - Incumbent: Doubly resonant AlN, LiNbO3, or GaAs microring second-harmonic generators requiring simultaneous dual-resonance alignment
  - Comparison: The singly resonant InGaP device tunes its SH output across 1.8 THz near 780 nm using only integrated heaters, and the paper states this 'matches the conversion efficiency of doubly resonant systems while exceeding their SH output power' (up to 14.4 mW at 57% absolute conversion efficiency in a 0.96 mm cavity). This is an open-loop thermal tuning demonstration at a fixed 20°C chip temperature with no frequency locking; using it as a metrology-grade wavelength-conversion module for atomic/ion clocks would require added frequency stabilization not shown here.

**Gap to practice.** The work is a single-chip, lab-bench demonstration: the SH pump for SPDC/OPO experiments is still generated off-chip using a separate PPLN doubling module referenced to a wavemeter, so no fully integrated pump-to-output chain has been shown. Coupling relies on lensed fibers and fiber arrays rather than packaged, connectorized interfaces, and the OPO exhibited occasional mode-hopping over 10-minute windows due to unstabilized fiber-to-chip coupling. Only a few individual devices (2.86 mm and 0.96 mm cavity lengths, three DC lengths) were characterized, so wafer-scale yield and device-to-device reproducibility are unestablished. The authors note measured nonlinear efficiency is below the theoretical maximum due to film-thickness variations causing phase-mismatch accumulation, so further gains require adaptive fabrication or tuning. An on-chip 780 nm pump laser is proposed as future work but not demonstrated. Two of the analysis's original headline numbers (exact intrinsic Q-factor, exact SHG %/W efficiency) are not recoverable from the supplied text and should not be cited until confirmed against the published PDF/figures. Overall this is early-stage component-level physics validation, not a packaged or manufacturable subsystem.

### Portable Vector NV-Diamond Magnetometer for Shot-Noise-Limited, Drift-Free Operation in Unshielded Environments

Annirudh K P, Vinayak Rane, Shradha Atakar, Sanika Joshi, et al. — [arXiv:2609.16901](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.16901) — TRL 4 — confidence medium — score 2.1

**Finding.** The authors built a lab-bench portable NV-diamond vector magnetometer with a digital signal-processing architecture addressing two known stability problems: (1) short-timescale laser noise leaking into the magnetic readout, and (2) multi-hour drift caused by temperature-induced changes in the bias magnets. Using an adaptive digital filter to match a post-diamond optical reference to the fluorescence channel, plus a second-derivative-based slope tracker to keep the frequency-to-field conversion calibrated as laser power drifts, they drove the off-resonance noise floor to within 1% of the calculated shot-noise/electronic limit (Foff=1.0±0.1). They then introduced a 'magnetic eigenvector transformation' -- an online covariance-based decomposition of the two Zeeman-split resonance frequencies -- that separates external magnetic signal from bias-magnet drift caused by ambient temperature swings, without an auxiliary thermometer. In a single 6-hour unshielded indoor lab run with temperature control off (22-26°C swing), this cut long-term drift roughly 10x (80 nT to 8 nT OADEV at τ=2000s) while preserving short-term sensitivity (~294 pT/√Hz at τ=1s) and real transient signals (a passing elevator). A companion 12-hour test over a wider 28-32°C range showed the secondary laser-drift correction becoming unreliable, so only the primary eigenvector step remained effective at larger thermal excursions.

**Operating constraints.** Uses a 3×3×0.5 mm3, 12C-enriched ensemble-NV diamond mounted on a custom Ω-shaped microwave antenna -- a single hand-built sensor head, not a mass-produced part; Requires a fiber-coupled 520 nm laser at up to ~363 mW incident power and associated optics (collimator, multiple lenses, dichroic filters, two photodetectors); Static bias field of ~1 mT from symmetrically positioned permanent magnets whose temperature dependence is the very drift source being corrected; Six simultaneously generated frequency-modulated microwave tones synthesized via FPGA/SoC (AMD Zynq-7020) with external IQ mixer and local oscillator near 2.87 GHz -- non-trivial RF hardware; Digital processing pipeline (FPGA-based lock-in demodulation, adaptive FIR/NLMS filtering, covariance diagonalization, α–β tracking) requires significant onboard computation; Demonstrated only in an unshielded but still indoor laboratory environment, not outdoors or in a vibration/EMI-rich industrial, airborne, or field setting; 12-hour test at 28-32°C showed the secondary second-derivative laser-drift correction became unreliable due to laser mode/power instability, leaving only partial (eigenvector-only) drift suppression; Requires two-photodetector balanced/differential optical readout with careful post-diamond beam separation, not a simple single-detector design; Coefficients and calibration (FIR filter initialization, phase/frequency/slope calibration per transition) performed via off-resonance calibration sweeps before each measurement session; Only a single continuous run of 6 hours (plus one 12-hour test) has been reported; no multi-unit reproducibility or long-duration (days/weeks) field data exists

**Applications vs. incumbents**

- *Quantum hardware engineering* — Field-deployable vector magnetometer core for portable quantum sensing instruments (e.g., geomagnetic survey or biomagnetic sensing platforms built on NV-diamond)
  - Incumbent: State-of-the-art fiber-coupled ensemble NV-diamond magnetometers with hardware-based common-mode balanced photodetection and Zeeman-split-pair tracking (e.g., Schloss et al. 2018, Patel et al. 2020, Graham et al. 2023)
  - Comparison: This work reports off-resonance noise factor F_off = 1.0±0.1 (i.e., at the calculated shot-noise/electronic limit) versus incumbent systems in the same benchmark table ranging from F_off = 1.3 (Sekiguchi 2024) to F_off = 14.0 (Stürner 2021); on-resonance F_on = 1.2±0.1 versus 1.5-13.2 for others. However, absolute sensitivity (~288-353 pT/√Hz per axis) is not better than the best fiber-coupled systems cited (e.g., Graham et al. 2023 report 30 pT/√Hz unshielded), so the improvement is in noise-floor closeness to the fundamental limit and drift suppression, not in raw sensitivity.
- *Quantum hardware engineering* — Software-only drift correction for stationary, indoor unshielded NV-magnetometer installations (e.g., lab or building-based long-duration magnetic monitoring) without an auxiliary temperature sensor
  - Incumbent: NV-magnetometer with auxiliary thermometer near the bias magnet and calibrated temperature-to-field correction (as in a recent field-deployed implementation, Halde et al. 2025)
  - Comparison: The eigenvector method reduces long-term drift roughly 10-fold (from ~80 nT to ~8 nT OADEV at τ=2000s) purely in software, without needing a thermometer or calibration matrix, but this has been demonstrated only in a single 6-hour indoor unshielded lab run; a companion 12-hour test at a wider 28-32°C range showed the secondary laser-drift correction becoming unreliable, so only partial suppression held up outside the tested 22-26°C window. No comparable numeric drift figure for a thermometer-corrected incumbent is given, so any advantage claimed is qualitative, and true field/outdoor deployment (vibration, longer duration, unattended operation) has not been shown.

**Gap to practice.** This is a single hand-built lab prototype validated only in one unshielded indoor laboratory over test windows of a few hours (a fully successful 6-hour run and a partially successful 12-hour run at a wider temperature range). There is no demonstration of packaging into a rugged field, airborne, or factory-floor enclosure, no reproducibility data across multiple diamond/antenna assemblies, and no outdoor or vibration/EMI-stressed testing. The 12-hour wider-temperature-range test already showed the second-derivative laser-drift correction becoming unreliable, indicating the current algorithm is not yet robust across the full range of real-world thermal and laser-mode conditions -- an aerospace or field-navigation application was removed from this analysis for exactly this reason, since the sensor head still depends on bench optics and has no vibration or EMI testing. Absolute per-axis sensitivity (~300 pT/√Hz) is also not yet competitive with best-in-class fiber-coupled NV systems reporting 30 pT/√Hz, so sensitivity gains are still needed alongside the demonstrated noise-floor and drift improvements before this could displace incumbent field magnetometers on sensitivity grounds.

### Phase-stable voltage bias for Josephson photonics devices

**Finding.** The authors built a single superconducting chip that combines two Josephson-junction devices: a Josephson voltage standard (JVS, normally used for metrology) and an inelastic Cooper-pair tunneling amplifier (ICTA, a dc-voltage-biased parametric amplifier).
