## 1. Graphene & 2D material production
**Paper:** [Electrically tunable moiré hybridization in scalable twist-with-a-spacer structures](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.02471) — Ian Babich et al.
**Pipeline:** Extreme material property -> Functional or structural material
**Readiness score:** 10/10 — weakest: operating_conditions (Operates at cryogenic conditions standard for moiré superlattices.)
**What they found:** Scientists stacked two sheets of graphene with a thin spacer layer of misaligned graphene in between, instead of putting them directly on top of each other. This spacer let them control the special electronic patterns (called moiré patterns) using an electric field, and it also kept the structure stable even at tiny twist angles of about 0.1 degree. They even grew these structures using a scalable chemical method (CVD) and got the same results as when built by hand.
**Why it matters for Graphene & 2D material production:** Right now, making these twisted graphene structures requires careful, slow, manual assembly, which limits how many can be made. This shows a way to mass-produce them with a standard industrial growth process, which could make advanced graphene-based electronics much more practical to manufacture.
**The catch:** The devices were still made and tested in a lab, so it's unclear if this CVD method works at the large industrial scale needed for commercial chips.

## 2. Indium refining
**Paper:** [Cooling-path-selected self-assembly of telecom InAsP quantum dots](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.01214) — Yiteng Zhang et al.
**Pipeline:** New fabrication or growth technique -> Manufacturing process
**Readiness score:** 10/10 — weakest: reproducibility (20 wafers with consistent results; good reproducibility shown.)
**What they found:** Scientists found a way to grow tiny light-emitting dots made of indium, arsenic and phosphorus on a chip, without actually depositing the dot material directly. Instead, they control how the chip cools down after heating it in arsenic gas, and that cooling path decides whether the dots form and what color of light they give off. Across 20 test wafers, they could predict the light's wavelength (between 1.2 and 1.6 micrometers, the range used in fiber-optic telecom) and got single-photon emission working up to 100 Kelvin, which is about minus 173 degrees Celsius.
**Why it matters for Indium refining:** This gives a more controllable recipe for making quantum light sources directly on indium phosphide chips, which matters for building quantum computers and secure communication devices using telecom-friendly wavelengths.
**The catch:** The single-photon emission only worked up to 100 Kelvin, far colder than room temperature, so these chips still need serious refrigeration to work at all.

## 3. Small-format lithium cells
**Paper:** [How much data does a memory kernel need? Measurement requirements for fractional models of battery and corrosion degradation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05426) — Conrard Giresse Tetsassi Feugmo et al.
**Pipeline:** Theory or model insight -> Design and simulation tools
**Readiness score:** 10/10 — weakest: reproducibility (Validated across two mechanistic models and de Levie model.)
**What they found:** Scientists tried to use fancy 'memory' math (called fractional calculus) to predict how batteries wear out and metals rust over time. They found you need about 3.1 decades of data to trust these models, but most real battery tests only cover 1.2 to 2.2 decades, so the usual data isn't enough. When they had enough data though, a better memory model raised the fit score from 0.862 to 0.998, almost perfect.
**Why it matters for Small-format lithium cells:** This tells battery makers that their current testing methods may not collect enough data to properly model how batteries degrade over long time periods, which could affect how accurately they predict battery lifespan.
**The catch:** This is a mathematical analysis of existing public battery datasets, not a new battery technology or even new experiments, so it tells people what data they need to collect in the future rather than giving them better batteries today.

## 4. Superconducting electronics & detectors
**Paper:** [Resetting a Superconducting Qubit with a Compact Colder Resistive Dissipator below the Qubit Frequency](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06216) — Jiajia Tian et al.  *(unverified — the checking pass failed for this paper)*
**Pipeline:** Coherence or entanglement improvement -> Quantum hardware
**Readiness score:** 10/10 — weakest: reproducibility (Single paper on one device platform)
**What they found:** Scientists built a tiny on-chip device that cools down a superconducting qubit (a quantum computer's basic memory bit) faster, without needing extra wires. It lowered the qubit's effective temperature from 49 to 35 thousandths of a degree above absolute zero, and the reset worked with a very low error rate of 0.28%.
**Why it matters for Superconducting electronics & detectors:** Fast, reliable resetting of qubits is needed to build bigger, more trustworthy quantum computers, and this method needs no extra control wiring, which matters a lot when you're trying to wire up thousands of qubits on a chip.
**The catch:** This was tested on a small number of qubits in a specialized lab fridge colder than outer space, and it's unclear how the dissipator design holds up when scaled to the large, densely packed qubit arrays needed for real quantum processors.

## 5. Mature-node chip foundry (28nm+)
**Paper:** [Jackknife-conformal uncertainty for linear ACE potentials: calibrated forces and silicon properties compared with Bayesian and POPS baselines](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08678) — James R. Kermode et al.
**Pipeline:** Theory or model insight -> Design and simulation tools
**Readiness score:** 10/10 — weakest: operating_conditions (Software tool for uncertainty quantification vs incumbent software.)
**What they found:** Scientists tested a new way to estimate how much to trust computer simulations that predict silicon's physical properties, like its stretchiness or how it behaves near cracks and defects. Their method, using a statistical trick called a 'jackknife,' gave more honest error bars than the usual approach, and a bigger model's predictions were about 4 times more precise than a smaller one.
**Why it matters for Mature-node chip foundry (28nm+):** Better-calibrated computer models of silicon could help chipmakers trust simulations instead of running costly physical tests, speeding up material design.
**The catch:** The method still fails for certain defects, like rearranged atoms near point defects, where it needs a special fix added after the fact because the bias is invisible to the normal check. It also misses some cases outright, like screw dislocations left out of training, which the smaller model under-covers.

## 6. Optical fiber & preforms
**Paper:** [Experimental demonstration of response-enhanced fiber Sagnac gyroscopy via imaginary weak-value amplification](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.00481) — Jing-Hui Huang et al.  *(unverified — the checking pass failed for this paper)*
**Pipeline:** Environmental sensitivity -> Sensing
**Readiness score:** 9/10 — weakest: reproducibility (not stated)
**What they found:** Scientists built a spinning sensor made from a 100-meter coil of optical fiber that detects rotation using light. By cleverly filtering the light's polarization (like only letting through light pointed in a very specific direction), they boosted the signal strength about 13 times and made the measurement 6.6 times steadier.
**Why it matters for Optical fiber & preforms:** This could make fiber-based rotation sensors, used in navigation systems for planes, ships, and robots, far more sensitive without needing new or expensive fiber, just smarter light filtering.
**The catch:** The long-term accuracy is still limited by flickering in the light source itself, so the gains in sensitivity do not yet translate into better stability over long periods.

## 7. Fiber & diode lasers
**Paper:** [Optical spectral asymmetry enables differential refractive-index sensing under near-perfect common-mode suppression in a single-cavity dual-comb fiber laser](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.02150) — Masayuki Higaki et al.
**Pipeline:** Environmental sensitivity -> Sensing
**Readiness score:** 9/10 — weakest: reproducibility (not stated)
**What they found:** Scientists built a laser that sends two beams of light looping around the same loop of fiber in opposite directions, like two cars racing around a track in opposite lanes. When they add a tiny sensor in the loop, they can measure how much liquid or gas changes the fiber's refractive index by comparing the tiny differences in each beam's 'ticking rate'. This trick cuts false signals from temperature changes by more than 100 times compared to older two-loop designs.
**Why it matters for Fiber & diode lasers:** Fiber laser systems used for precision sensing and instrumentation could become far more stable against temperature drift, which is one of the biggest headaches in real-world deployment.
**The catch:** The improved stability comes at a cost: the sensor is less sensitive to the actual thing it's measuring because it shares one sensor between both beams, and the paper only reports lab results, not field tests outside a controlled setup.

## 8. Solar PV cells & modules
**Paper:** [Edge photocurrent distribution in luminescent solar concentrators: analytical treatment and solar cell arrangement](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.02668) — Ilya Sychugov et al.  *(unverified — the checking pass failed for this paper)*
**Pipeline:** Theory or model insight -> Design and simulation tools
**Readiness score:** 9/10 — weakest: performance_margin (Model reproduces known mismatch; 8-12% loss matches incumbent problem.)
**What they found:** Luminescent solar concentrators are panels that catch sunlight and funnel it sideways to small solar cells on the edges, like a window that gathers light and shines it into a flashlight at the corner. The light hitting different spots along the edge isn't even, so the cells connected in a chain don't all make the same current, and that mismatch can waste 8-12% of the power. The researchers built a math formula that predicts this unevenness and matched real measurements within 2-3%.
**Why it matters for Solar PV cells & modules:** This gives solar panel designers a quick way to figure out where to place edge cells so they lose less power to mismatch, without having to run slow computer simulations every time.
**The catch:** This only applies to the niche luminescent solar concentrator design, not the ordinary silicon solar panels that make up almost all the solar market today.

## 9. Titanium sponge
**Paper:** [Effect of molybdenum content on phase transformations during continuous heating of metastable β Ti-Mo alloys](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04858) — Veronika Kočiščáková et al.  *(unverified — the checking pass failed for this paper)*
**Pipeline:** Theory or model insight -> Design and simulation tools
**Readiness score:** 9/10 — weakest: performance_margin (Maps phase diagram; no quantitative property improvement stated.)
**What they found:** Scientists heated three titanium-molybdenum alloys slowly and watched what happened inside the metal using several different tools at once, like checking a cake with a thermometer, a toothpick, and a timer all together. They found that adding more molybdenum delays and shrinks the window where an unwanted in-between crystal structure (called omega phase) forms before the metal settles into its final structure.
**Why it matters for Titanium sponge:** Titanium alloy makers use heat treatments to control the internal structure of titanium parts, which determines strength and flexibility; knowing exactly how molybdenum content changes this process helps engineers design better aerospace-grade titanium alloys.
**The catch:** This is basic science on lab-made alloy samples under one specific heating rate, not a new production method, so it won't change how titanium sponge or mill products are actually manufactured.

## 10. Power adapters & supplies
**Paper:** [CMS ECAL Phase-2 upgrade: characterisation and stability analysis of a three-stage low-voltage power-conversion system](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06807) — Liv Anke Richter et al.
**Pipeline:** Better isolation or control -> Enabling infrastructure
**Readiness score:** 9/10 — weakest: performance_margin (Stabilization marginal; 0.76-0.79 distance to critical point matches incumbent.)
**What they found:** Scientists tested a three-stage power supply system built for a giant particle detector to make sure it doesn't become unstable, like checking that a row of dominoes won't topple itself. They measured how the different converter stages interact and found both connection points were stable. They also found that when the system turns on, charging capacitors can cause a current spike, so they built two circuit board designs that turn things on in a staggered order to keep the current safely under 5 amps, well within the 8 amp safety limit.
**Why it matters for Power adapters & supplies:** This is a careful engineering check for a specific scientific instrument, showing how to avoid power-supply instability and inrush current problems in multi-stage conversion systems.
**The catch:** This was designed and tested for one very specific particle-physics detector application, not as a general improvement to commercial power adapters or supplies.
