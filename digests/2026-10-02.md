## 1. Mature-node chip foundry (28nm+)
**Paper:** [Efficient simulation of millimeter-scale complex-modulated integrated Bragg gratings via hierarchical locally periodic eigenmode expansion](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.30224) — Rui Cheng et al.  *(unverified — the checking pass failed for this paper)*
**Pipeline:** Theory or model insight -> Design and simulation tools
**Readiness score:** 10/10 — weakest: reproducibility (Experimental validation across multiple complex-modulated designs.)
**What they found:** Scientists built a faster computer simulation method for designing tiny mirror-like structures called Bragg gratings, which are etched into silicon chips to control light. Their method, called HLP-EME, predicted how light would bounce off these structures over 1,000 times faster than the standard simulation technique, cutting simulation time from potentially days to just a few minutes, while still matching real experimental measurements.
**Why it matters for Mature-node chip foundry (28nm+):** Faster, more accurate simulation tools let chip designers create better silicon photonic devices (chips that use light instead of electricity to send data) more quickly, speeding up design cycles for mature-node silicon photonics manufacturing.
**The catch:** This is a design and simulation tool, not a new manufacturing process or material, so it speeds up how engineers plan chips but doesn't change how the chips themselves are actually fabricated.

## 2. Gallium refining
**Paper:** [Enabling the Ambient Pressure Growth of ScB2 Crystals for AlGaN Power Electronics](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.36493) — Satya K. Kushwaha et al.
**Pipeline:** New fabrication or growth technique -> Manufacturing process
**Readiness score:** 9/10 — weakest: performance_margin (First ambient-pressure ScB2 growth; substrate comparison to incumbent not quantified.)
**What they found:** Scientists grew single crystals of a hard ceramic called ScB2 using a technique that melts a thin zone of material with lasers, done at normal air pressure instead of needing special high-pressure equipment. The best crystals, grown from a boron-rich starting mix, came out as one clean block with no cracks or grain boundaries, and their surface showed a work function of about 5 electron-volts, matching what's expected for a highly conductive material.
**Why it matters for Gallium refining:** ScB2 could serve as a substrate that matches the atomic spacing of AlGaN, a material used to build power electronics chips, which would let manufacturers grow better, more defect-free chip layers on top of it.
**The catch:** The crystals were grown slowly, at only 0.2 to 2 millimeters per hour, and the abstract does not show these substrates actually being used to grow working AlGaN power transistors yet.

## 3. Small-format lithium cells
**Paper:** [Site-Selective Yttrium Substitution in Ti3AlC2 Enables Interlayer Engineering and Li Transport in Ti3C2Tx cathodes for High-Power Energy Storage](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.32707) — Tetiana Boichuk et al.
**Pipeline:** Anomalous transport -> Energy and power
**Readiness score:** 9/10 — weakest: reproducibility (Not stated; single group demonstration.)
**What they found:** Scientists added a tiny bit of yttrium metal while making a material called MXene, which is used as a battery electrode. This made the material's internal layers a bit wider, letting lithium ions move faster, and the battery kept 74 mAh/g of its storage capacity even after 1000 fast-charge cycles, with a strong power output of almost 4000 W/kg.
**Why it matters for Small-format lithium cells:** This could give small lithium battery makers a cathode material that charges and discharges faster while lasting longer, which matters for devices like power tools or drones that need quick bursts of energy.
**The catch:** This is a lab-scale MXene cathode with modest total capacity (130 mAh/g), so it would need to beat established commercial cathode materials in cost and manufacturability before factories would switch to it.

## 4. Superconducting electronics & detectors
**Paper:** [Spiking neural networks for streaming qubit readout](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02129) — Barry M. Dillon et al.  *(unverified — the checking pass failed for this paper)*
**Pipeline:** New switching or state-storage mechanism -> Logic and memory
**Readiness score:** 9/10 — weakest: reproducibility (Single superconducting platform; independent group validation not stated.)
**What they found:** Scientists built a brain-inspired computer program (a spiking neural network) that reads the state of a superconducting qubit while the signal is still coming in, instead of waiting for the whole measurement to finish. It worked almost as well as a full-trace neural network and beat the older 'matched filter' method, and it ran fast enough on a chip (FPGA) to keep up with each new piece of data as it arrived.
**Why it matters for Superconducting electronics & detectors:** Quantum computers need to know a qubit's state instantly to fix errors or make decisions in real time, so a faster, more accurate readout method could speed up error correction and control loops in superconducting quantum processors.
**The catch:** This was tested on specific lab hardware and readout chunks, so it is not yet shown to work across the many qubits and noise conditions of a full-scale quantum computer.

## 5. Industrial forgings
**Paper:** [Evaluation of effective wave velocities in polycrystalline materials using the ultrasonic reflection matrix](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.26343) — Gatien Clément et al.
**Pipeline:** New measurement technique -> Inspection and quality control
**Readiness score:** 9/10 — weakest: performance_margin (Maps local elastic moduli; incremental over existing ultrasonic velocity methods.)
**What they found:** Scientists bounced sound waves off metal parts and used the echoes to map how stiff and springy the metal is, point by point. They could figure out properties like Young's modulus (how much something stretches under force) without cutting the part open. They tested this on real metal samples and a computer-simulated one with uneven properties to check accuracy.
**Why it matters for Industrial forgings:** Industries that make big metal castings and forgings need to check for weak spots or uneven quality inside metal parts, like checking a cake for bad batter without slicing it open. This method could let them check that from outside, without destroying the part.
**The catch:** The method was tested on lab samples and simulations, not on large real-world industrial parts with complex shapes, so it is unclear how well it scales to factory inspection lines.

## 6. Solar PV cells & modules
**Paper:** [Hamiltonian learning reveals optoelectronic mechanisms across thermodynamic state space in soft semiconductors](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.29361) — Frederik Vonhoff et al.  *(unverified — the checking pass failed for this paper)*
**Pipeline:** Theory or model insight -> Design and simulation tools
**Readiness score:** 9/10 — weakest: reproducibility (not stated)
**What they found:** Scientists built a computer modeling tool called FLOW-OTTER that predicts how a solar cell material called halide perovskite behaves as temperature and pressure change. It correctly predicted how the material's 'band gap' (which controls how it absorbs light) shifts with heat and squeezing, using only calculations made at everyday pressure. It also figured out the atomic-level reason the material reacts unevenly to pressure, tracing it to how lead and bromine atoms' electrons interact.
**Why it matters for Solar PV cells & modules:** This gives perovskite solar cell researchers a faster way to predict and understand how a promising solar material will perform under real-world heat and stress, without having to run expensive lab experiments or slow quantum calculations every time.
**The catch:** This is a software and simulation result, not a new material or device, so it still needs to be tested against real perovskite solar cells manufactured and aged under actual sunlight and weather.

## 7. Refractories
**Paper:** [Retrainable physics-integrated neural differentiable modeling of sintering across material systems](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.31518) — Zeping Chen et al.
**Pipeline:** Theory or model insight -> Design and simulation tools
**Readiness score:** 9/10 — weakest: reproducibility (not stated)
**What they found:** Researchers built a computer model that predicts how ceramic powders shrink and grow their internal grains when heated, called sintering. They tested it on three different materials and found it guessed the final density and grain size better than other computer models, with errors ranging from about 9% to 19%.
**Why it matters for Refractories:** Sintering is how most ceramics and refractory materials are made solid and strong, so a better prediction tool could help engineers pick the right temperature and time without running as many costly test furnaces.
**The catch:** The model was only tested on three specific lab materials with small, carefully measured datasets, and its uncertainty estimates were shown to be unreliable, meaning it might miss real-world surprises when applied to new materials or factory-scale batches.

## 8. Sputtering targets & thin-film deposition
**Paper:** [Quantitative Interleaved-Pulse Acquisition Extends the Range of Isotope-Ratio Measurements in ToF-SIMS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.37413) — Anton V. Ievlev et al.
**Pipeline:** New measurement technique -> Inspection and quality control
**Readiness score:** 9/10 — weakest: operating_conditions (Requires ToF-SIMS instrument; standard for isotope analysis but not portable.)
**What they found:** Scientists improved a microscope-like tool (ToF-SIMS) that counts different versions of atoms (isotopes) inside thin films. By mixing short and long measurement pulses together, they could count both a very common isotope and a very rare one at the same time, even when one is 500 times rarer than the other. This let them see rare isotopes 14 times more faintly than before, using 13 times fewer scans on simple test materials.
**Why it matters for Sputtering targets & thin-film deposition:** This makes it easier to check how atoms move through thin oxide and battery films, which matters for quality control when making coated materials like sputtering targets and thin-film devices.
**The catch:** This is a measurement upgrade for existing machines, not a new material or production process, so it speeds up lab analysis but does not change how the materials themselves are made.
