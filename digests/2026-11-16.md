## 1. Superconducting electronics & detectors
**Paper:** [Scalable operating currents in amorphous W-N/FePt superconducting rectifiers](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12476) — S. Russo et al.
**Pipeline:** Anomalous transport -> Energy and power
**Readiness score:** 10/10 — weakest: operating_conditions (4.1-4.3 K operation comparable to incumbent superconducting diode incumbents.)
**What they found:** Scientists built tiny superconducting wires layered with a magnetic material called FePt, and found that electric current flows much more easily in one direction than the other, without needing any outside magnet to make that happen. At the best setup, this one-way effect was about 75-80% efficient, and making the superconducting layer thicker widened the gap between the 'easy' and 'hard' current directions more than five times.
**Why it matters for Superconducting electronics & detectors:** Superconducting circuits that act like one-way valves (diodes) without needing clunky external magnets could make superconducting chips and detectors smaller and simpler to build.
**The catch:** The whole device only works when cooled to about 3-4 Kelvin, which is colder than outer space, and was only tested on microscopic lab bridges, not full-size circuits.

## 2. Solar PV cells & modules
**Paper:** [Analytical Framework Relating Capacitance-Frequency Measurements and Hysteresis in Perovskite Solar Cells](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12115) — Manjeet Kumar et al.
**Pipeline:** New measurement technique -> Inspection and quality control
**Readiness score:** 10/10 — weakest: reproducibility (Drift-diffusion simulations match experiments across wide parameter ranges; predictions confirmed experimentally.)
**What they found:** Scientists made a math formula that can figure out how ions (tiny charged particles) move inside perovskite solar cells just by measuring how the cell responds to different electrical frequencies, like tapping a bell at different speeds and listening to the ring. This is much faster than the old way, which needed long experiments and heavy computer simulations. They checked their formula against computer models and real lab measurements and it matched well.
**Why it matters for Solar PV cells & modules:** Perovskite solar cells often degrade or lose efficiency because these wandering ions cause a glitchy effect called hysteresis. A quick, simple way to measure ion movement helps engineers test and improve perovskite cells faster, speeding up their path to mass production.
**The catch:** This is a measurement and analysis tool, not a new solar cell material or design, so it helps researchers understand existing cells better but doesn't by itself make perovskite solar cells more efficient or durable.

## 3. Polysilicon
**Paper:** [Industrial RPCVD growth of high-purity 28Si epilayers with 29Si content below 30 ppm for high-fidelity electron spin qubits](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.11405) — Zhenzhen Kong et al.
**Pipeline:** Coherence or entanglement improvement -> Quantum hardware
**Readiness score:** 10/10 — weakest: operating_conditions (Silicon spin qubits; standard 1.5 K dilution-refrigerator temperature, same as incumbent.)
**What they found:** Scientists grew an extremely pure form of silicon, called 28Si, using a factory-style vapor deposition machine instead of a lab-only method. The silicon was 99.997% pure in the right atom type, with a nearly complete removal of a different silicon atom type that messes up quantum signals. Devices made from this material kept their quantum information intact for up to 28.8 microseconds and ran quantum operations correctly 99.9% of the time.
**Why it matters for Polysilicon:** This shows that a factory-ready machine can make the ultra-pure silicon needed for quantum computers, instead of relying on tiny, expensive one-off lab batches, which could help scale up quantum chip production.
**The catch:** The process only works below a narrow temperature window (under 950°C), and so far it has only been tested on small lab-scale wafers and devices, not full production lines.

## 4. Mature-node chip foundry (28nm+)
**Paper:** [Development and characterization of monolithic sensors with internal gain in a 110 nm CMOS process](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.15731) — G. Gioachin et al.
**Pipeline:** Narrow, stable transitions -> Timing and metrology
**Readiness score:** 10/10 — weakest: reproducibility (Multiple test beam campaigns and characterizations reported.)
**What they found:** Scientists built tiny light sensors on an ordinary 110 nanometer computer chip, the kind of chip factories already know how to make. These sensors can detect fast-moving particles and tell when they arrived with a precision of about 78 trillionths of a second, like a stopwatch that can split a second into billions of tiny slices.
**Why it matters for Mature-node chip foundry (28nm+):** This shows that normal chip factories, not special expensive ones, can make detectors for particle physics experiments, which could lower costs and simplify production for specialized sensor applications.
**The catch:** This only works for very specific scientific detector uses, like tracking particles in physics experiments, not for everyday chips, so it won't change how foundries make typical electronics.

## 5. Optical fiber & preforms
**Paper:** [Optical fiber shape sensing using picosecond spatiotemporal single-photon reflectometry](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16717) — Harikumar K Chandrasekharan et al.
**Pipeline:** Environmental sensitivity -> Sensing
**Readiness score:** 10/10 — weakest: operating_conditions (Optical fiber interrogated by single-photon avalanche diode array; room-temperature or cryogenic compatible, superior to lab-grade isolation incumbent.)
**What they found:** Scientists sent tiny flashes of light, down to single photons, into a specially engineered optical fiber and watched how the light bounced back over time. By studying the pattern and timing of that returning light, they could figure out the fiber's 3D shape just from one end, without needing special multi-core fibers or complex interference measurements. Their system was accurate, with low noise and less than 1% variation when repeating measurements.
**Why it matters for Optical fiber & preforms:** This could let people track the exact bending and shape of fibers used in medical tools, robots, or structural sensors, using only one end of a simple fiber instead of expensive specialized cables.
**The catch:** The method still relies on a specially 'laser-engineered' fiber and bulky time-resolved photon-counting equipment, so it is not yet a drop-in upgrade for ordinary optical fibers used in telecom or sensing networks.

## 6. Gallium refining
**Paper:** [$>3\text{ W/mm}$ Output Power $\mathrm{Al}_{0.61}\mathrm{Ga}_{0.39}\mathrm{N}$ Channel HEMT with $24.4\%$ Associated PAE at $10\text{ GHz}$](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.11042) — Meherab Hossain et al.
**Pipeline:** New switching or state-storage mechanism -> Logic and memory
**Readiness score:** 9/10 — weakest: reproducibility (Single fabrication run; independent replication by other groups not stated.)
**What they found:** Scientists built a tiny power transistor out of a special gallium-aluminum-nitrogen material with a very high aluminum content. At a radio frequency used for radar (10 GHz), it pushed out 3.07 watts of power per millimeter of width, and turned 24.4% of the electrical energy into usable radio power without wasting it as heat. A tweaked version even hit 33.9% efficiency at a bit lower power.
**Why it matters for Gallium refining:** This kind of ultra-wide-bandgap transistor could let gallium-based chips handle higher voltages and more power than current gallium nitride devices, which matters for radar, 5G, and military-grade radio transmitters that need compact, efficient power amplifiers.
**The catch:** This was a single lab-made device, not a manufacturable chip, and 'ultra-wide bandgap' materials like this are notoriously hard to make in large, defect-free, reliable batches.

## 7. Graphene & 2D material production
**Paper:** [Wafer-scale MoS2 and HZO-based Ferroelectric Field Effect Transistors with 4.4 V Memory Window for Back-End Compatible Multibit Memory](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12968) — Manisha Rajput et al.
**Pipeline:** New switching or state-storage mechanism -> Logic and memory
**Readiness score:** 9/10 — weakest: reproducibility (Wafer-scale devices; independent group replication not stated explicitly.)
**What they found:** Scientists built a memory switch across an entire wafer using a material called MoS2, which is only 2-3 atoms thick, stacked with a special memory oxide called HZO. This switch can store data as 4.4 volts' worth of distinct 'on' states, like a dimmer switch with many stops instead of just on/off, and it was 100,000 times better at telling on from off.
**Why it matters for Graphene & 2D material production:** If 2D materials like MoS2 can be made reliably across whole wafers, they could replace silicon in ultra-thin memory chips that stack on top of normal circuits, letting computer chips pack much more memory into the same space.
**The catch:** The chip was tested in a lab on a research wafer, so nobody yet knows if it can be made cheaply, reliably, and defect-free at the scale a real memory factory needs.

## 8. Small-format lithium cells
**Paper:** [A Machine-learning-aided Workflow for Sampling Small Polaron Dynamics in Energy Materials](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18041) — Tavinder Singh et al.
**Pipeline:** Anomalous transport -> Energy and power
**Readiness score:** 9/10 — weakest: reproducibility (Single material system tested; independent replication not stated.)
**What they found:** Scientists used a computer shortcut (machine learning trained on a smaller set of detailed physics calculations) to predict how electrons hop through a battery material called LTO. They only had to do 154 of the hardest calculations instead of 3649, a 20-fold speedup, and still correctly predicted the material's electrical conductivity, matching real lab measurements.
**Why it matters for Small-format lithium cells:** This gives battery researchers a faster way to figure out how well candidate electrode materials will conduct electricity, without needing to make and test them all in the lab first.
**The catch:** This is purely a computer simulation method for one already-known material, not a new battery material or manufacturing process, so it does not change what gets built or how it's made.

## 9. Flat glass
**Paper:** [Boosting bottom-up milling of glass with GHz laser bursts](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12354) — Miglė Mackevičiūtė et al.
**Pipeline:** New fabrication or growth technique -> Manufacturing process
**Readiness score:** 8/10 — weakest: integration (laser-based, compatibility with standard fab not addressed)
**What they found:** Scientists used fast laser pulses, fired in rapid bursts like a tiny jackhammer, to cut away glass from underneath instead of the usual top-down way. They found a sweet spot (20 or more pulses per burst) that removed more than 250 cubic micrometers of glass per tiny bit of laser energy, and could clear material as fast as 8 cubic millimeters per second.
**Why it matters for Flat glass:** Precise, fast laser milling could let glass makers cut or shape glass parts (like screens or optical pieces) more efficiently without cracking or wasting material.
**The catch:** This was tested on small lab samples of soda-lime glass and fused silica, so it is not yet shown to work at the scale or speed needed for full factory production lines.
